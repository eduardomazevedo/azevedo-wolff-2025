\subsection{Discussion of Assumptions, Limitations, and Counter-Examples}
\label{sec:counter-examples}
\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Safe Score Region.}}
Another crucial assumption is that there exists a large enough region of safe scores, where $f_{aa}(y|a) \geq 0$ for all $y$ with scores below a threshold. The theorem makes this requirement precise with the capacity and convexity conditions.


Increasing score (also known as monotone likelihood ratio property) is a critical assumption, without which the first-order approach often fails. The importance of the assumption is easy to see with the fat-tailed Student-$t$ distribution. The $t$ distribution is similar to a Gaussian, but has fatter tails. As a consequence, the score is similar to the Gaussian score close to the mean, but approaches zero in both tails (table \ref{tab:dists}). Figure \ref{fig:t-log-pp} displays an example where the first-order approach is invalid for any reservation wage where principal profits are positive.

\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Sufficient Risk Aversion.}}
Risk aversion is an important assumption. In the risk-neutral case, there are simple counterexamples where the first-order approach fails even when the distributional conditions of Theorem \ref{thm:main} are satisfied. Supplementary Appendix \ref{sec:risk-neutral-counterexample} gives a three-state example where the first-order approach fails at arbitrarily high reservation utilities.

For practical purposes, the first-order approach can be valid with considerably lower risk aversion than the sufficient conditions in Theorem \ref{thm:main} require. In the CRRA case, Theorem \ref{thm:main} covers coefficients of relative risk aversion of $1$ or more. Supplementary Appendix \ref{sec:asymptotic-affinity}, Proposition \ref{prop:asymptotic-affinity}, gives a sufficient condition that works for every positive coefficient of relative risk aversion below $1$, at the cost of stronger distributional assumptions. The main additional assumption is concavity of the expected score, $a\mapsto\E_a[s(Y)]$, together with moment bounds. Corollary \ref{cor:gaussian-crra-extension} shows that this extension applies to Gaussian, Poisson, exponential, gamma, geometric, Bernoulli, and binomial output under the same parameter restrictions used in Remark \ref{rem:safe-tails-common-distributions}.


\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Limited liability.}}
Limited liability is crucial to guarantee existence of an optimal contract. The intuition is clear from the Lagrangian's first order condition in equation (\ref{eq:foc}). When the right-hand side is negative, it is optimal to give the agent the lowest possible $w(y)$. This only has a solution with limited liability and $u(w_0) > - \infty$. This is why with log utility our assumptions require $w_0 > 0$. When the score is unbounded below, a negative right-hand side happens for any $\mu >0$, this existence problem is extremely common without limited liability. These existence issues are well-known since \textcite{mirrlees1999}. The reason why we impose limited liability is to guarantee existence and not to guarantee the validity of the first-order approach. In fact, limited liability generates the kink in the optimal contract and makes it more difficult to guarantee the validity of the first-order approach.
