\subsection{Discussion of Assumptions, Limitations, and Counterexamples}
\label{sec:counter-examples}
\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Numerical Experiments.}}
Figure~\ref{fig:foa-principal-summary} summarizes the validity of the first-order approach across our numerical principal's problems.  Except for the Student-$t$ cases, the range of reservation wages over which the first-order approach fails is tiny: it either holds at the monopsony wage or becomes valid after only a small increase.  This pattern survives substantial changes in noise, initial wealth, and effort costs, and relative risk aversion as low as $0.25$.  By contrast, the Student-$t$ distribution has an empty $\mathcal Q_{\mathrm{safe}}$, and the first-order approach can fail throughout the economically relevant range.  The same conclusions hold in the fixed-action cost-minimization experiments reported in Supplementary Appendix~\ref{sec:foa-numerical-experiments}.

\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Safe Score Region.}}
A key assumption of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is the availability of a sufficiently large safe low-score region, encoded by $\mathcal Q_{\mathrm{safe}}$.  The region must be large enough for the required conditions on $\mathrm{Curv}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ and $\mathrm{Cap}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ to hold.

In many common distributions, the score is increasing in $y$, and $f_{aa}(y\mid a)\geq0$ in the left tail.  This is true for Gaussian, Poisson, exponential, gamma, geometric, Bernoulli, and binomial distributions under the parameter restrictions in Remark~\ref{rem:safe-tails-common-distributions}.  Thus, although the theorem does not formally require monotonicity of the score, many common examples with a nontrivial safe low-score region have increasing scores.

To see the importance of this region, consider the Student-$t$ distribution.  The $t$ distribution resembles a Gaussian but has fatter tails.  Consequently, its score resembles the Gaussian score near the mean but approaches zero in both tails (Table~\ref{tab:dists}).  The score is not monotone and there may be no nonempty safe low-score region.%
\footnote{Let $y^*:=a_0-\sigma\sqrt{d}$, where the intended-action score attains its minimum; every nonempty score sublevel set contains $y^*$.  Moreover,
\[
 \frac{f_{aa}(y\mid a)}{f(y\mid a)}
 =\frac{(d+1)[(d+2)(y-a)^2-d\sigma^2]}
 {[d\sigma^2+(y-a)^2]^2}.
\]
If $y^*\in\mathcal A$, evaluating at $a=y^*$ gives
$f_{aa}(y^*\mid y^*)/f(y^*\mid y^*)=-(d+1)/(d\sigma^2)<0$, independently of the parameter values.}

While distributions with an empty $\mathcal Q_{\mathrm{safe}}$ lead to common failures of the first-order approach, we note that the theorem's exact conditions on $\mathrm{Curv}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ and $\mathrm{Cap}_{\mathrm{safe}}$ are sufficient but not necessary, and often not tight. For example, in the CRRA and CARA cases covered by Corollary~\ref{cor:common-utilities}, part (i), the first-order approach holds for values of initial wealth $w_0$ that are not low enough to satisfy the capacity condition in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, part (i). The reason is that the theorem only gives sufficient conditions and, moreover, relies on the concavity of $U(w,a)$ in $a$, whereas sometimes the first-order approach is valid even if $U(w,a)$ is not concave in $a$.


\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Sufficient Risk Aversion.}}
Risk aversion is an important assumption. In the risk-neutral case, there are simple counterexamples where the first-order approach fails even when the distributional conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} are satisfied. Supplementary Appendix~\ref{sec:risk-neutral-counterexample} gives a three-state example where the first-order approach fails at arbitrarily high reservation utilities.

For practical purposes, the first-order approach can be valid with considerably lower risk aversion than the sufficient conditions in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} require. In the CRRA case, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} covers coefficients of relative risk aversion of $1$ or more. Supplementary Appendix~\ref{sec:asymptotic-affinity}, Corollary~\ref{cor:asymptotic-affinity-foa}, gives a sufficient condition that works for every positive coefficient of relative risk aversion below $1$, at the cost of stronger distributional assumptions. The main additional assumption is concavity of the expected score, $a\mapsto\E_a[s(Y)]$, together with moment bounds. Corollary~\ref{cor:gaussian-crra-extension} shows that this extension applies to Gaussian, Poisson, exponential, gamma, geometric, Bernoulli, and binomial output under the same parameter restrictions used in Remark~\ref{rem:safe-tails-common-distributions}.


\medskip\noindent\textit{\textbf{Limited liability.}}
In our setting, limited liability is crucial to guarantee existence of an optimal contract. The intuition is clear from the Lagrangian's first-order condition in equation~\eqref{eq:foc}. When the right-hand side is negative, it is optimal to give the agent the lowest possible $w(y)$. This only has a solution with limited liability and $u(w_0) > - \infty$. This is why with log utility our assumptions require $w_0 > 0$. When the score is unbounded below, the right-hand side is negative for any $\mu >0$; thus, this existence problem is extremely common without limited liability. These existence issues have been well known since \textcite{mirrlees1999}. Other settings can instead obtain existence from bounded scores, bounded contracts, or related restrictions. The reason why we impose limited liability is to guarantee existence and not to guarantee the validity of the first-order approach. In fact, limited liability generates the kink in the optimal contract and makes it more difficult to guarantee the validity of the first-order approach.
