# Plan to Generalize Proposition 1: Existence and Characterization of the Relaxed Solution

This note records the plan for generalizing Proposition 1 separately from the concavity result. The goal is to get as much generality as possible for the relaxed problem essentially for free, while keeping the paper's main result focused on concavity.

Main changes:

1. allow utility to be bounded above;
2. allow discrete output distributions such as Poisson;
3. state the result for densities with respect to a common dominating measure on a common support;
4. replace ``reservation utility goes to infinity'' by ``reservation utility approaches the maximum feasible relaxed utility'' when utility is bounded.

Throughout, the set of possible outputs remains a subset of the real line. This is enough for all examples in the paper.

---

## 1. Output space and common dominating measure

Let the output space be a fixed set

$$
\mathcal Y\subseteq \mathbb R.
$$

For every action $a$, output has density $f(\cdot|a)$ with respect to a common dominating measure $\nu$ on $\mathcal Y$. Expectations are written as

$$
\int_{\mathcal Y} h(y)f(y|a)\,d\nu(y).
$$

This covers continuous output on $\mathbb R$ or $[0,\infty)$, finite-support output, and countable-support output such as Poisson. In the continuous cases $\nu$ is Lebesgue measure; in the discrete cases $\nu$ is counting measure.

Maintain common support: $\{y\in\mathcal Y:f(y|a)>0\}$ is independent of $a$. In practice, take this common support to be $\mathcal Y$ and assume

$$
f(y|a)>0
\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal Y,\ a\in\mathcal A.
$$

This avoids action-dependent-support complications and makes the score well-defined:

$$
S(y|a):=\partial_a\log f(y|a).
$$

At the intended action $a_0$, the local incentive constraint is

$$
\int_{\mathcal Y}v(y)S(y|a_0)f(y|a_0)\,d\nu(y)=c'(a_0).
$$

Under the regularity condition allowing differentiation under the integral sign,

$$
\int_{\mathcal Y}S(y|a_0)f(y|a_0)\,d\nu(y)=0.
$$

---

## 2. Utility may be bounded above

Replace the current assumption $\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)=\infty$ with

$$
\bar u:=\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)\in(u(0),\infty].
$$

Continue to assume that $u$ is strictly increasing, strictly concave, smooth, and

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}u'(x)=0.
$$

The compensation cost function is still $k=u^{-1}$ on the range

$$
u([0,\infty))=[u(0),\bar u).
$$

If $\bar u<\infty$, then

$$
k(v)\to\infty
\quad\text{as}\quad
v\uparrow\bar u.
$$

The link function remains

$$
g(z):=k'^{-1}\left(\max\left\{\frac1{u'(0)},z\right\}\right).
$$

When utility is bounded, $g(z)\to\bar u$. When utility is unbounded, $g(z)\to\infty$.

---

## 3. Implementability condition for Proposition 1

With bounded utility, not every intended action is locally implementable. The maximum local incentive obtainable from a utility contract with range in $[u(0),\bar u]$ is finite.

For Proposition 1, a clean sufficient condition is

$$
(\bar u-u(0))
\int_{\{S(y|a_0)>0\}}S(y|a_0)f(y|a_0)\,d\nu(y)
>
c'(a_0).
\tag{Imp}
$$

Equivalently, since $E_{a_0}[S(Y|a_0)]=0$,

$$
(\bar u-u(0))E_{a_0}[S_+]>c'(a_0).
$$

If $\bar u=\infty$, this condition is automatic provided the score has positive mass above zero and $c'(a_0)>0$.

This condition is intentionally weaker than the tail-safe capacity condition needed for the concavity theorem. It only says that the available utility spread is large enough to generate the required local incentive at $a_0$.

A more precise version could allow thresholds in score space. For example, a step contract with low utility below a score threshold $r$ and high utility above it generates incentive

$$
(\bar u-u(0))\int_{\{S>r\}}S f_0\,d\nu.
$$

But the simple $r=0$ sufficient condition is enough for the relaxed-solution proposition.

## 4. Canonical contracts and the LIC multiplier

For $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R$, define the canonical contract as before:

$$
V(y|\lambda,\mu)=g\bigl(\lambda+\mu S(y|a_0)\bigr).
$$

For fixed $\lambda$, define the incentive generated by $\mu$:

$$
I(\lambda,\mu):=
\int_{\mathcal Y}V(y|\lambda,\mu)S(y|a_0)f(y|a_0)\,d\nu(y).
$$

The local incentive constraint is

$$
I(\lambda,\tilde\mu(\lambda))=c'(a_0).
$$

Key facts:

1. $I(\lambda,0)=0$ because $V(y|\lambda,0)$ is constant and $E[S]=0$.
2. $I(\lambda,\mu)$ is weakly increasing in $\mu$.
3. $I(\lambda,\mu)$ is strictly increasing at any solution of LIC, under a mild nondegeneracy condition on the score.
4. For fixed $\lambda$,

$$
\lim_{\mu\to\infty}g(\lambda+\mu S(y|a_0))
=
\begin{cases}
\bar u, & S(y|a_0)>0,\\
g(\lambda), & S(y|a_0)=0,\\
u(0), & S(y|a_0)<0.
\end{cases}
$$

Since states with $S=0$ do not contribute to the incentive integral,

$$
\lim_{\mu\to\infty}I(\lambda,\mu)
=
(\bar u-u(0))
\int_{\{S>0\}}S f_0\,d\nu.
$$

Under (Imp), this limit exceeds $c'(a_0)$, so for every $\lambda$ there exists a unique

$$
\tilde\mu(\lambda)>0
$$

solving LIC.

This is the generalized version of the current Lemma \ref{lem:mu-tilde}.

## 5. Relaxed Pareto frontier

Define

$$
\tilde V(y|\lambda):=V(y|\lambda,\tilde\mu(\lambda)).
$$

Define

$$
\tilde U(\lambda):=
\int \tilde V(y|\lambda)f(y|a_0)\,d\nu(y)-c(a_0),
$$

and

$$
\tilde W(\lambda):=
\int k(\tilde V(y|\lambda))f(y|a_0)\,d\nu(y).
$$

The same convex-duality/Pareto argument as in the current proof gives:

* for each $\lambda$, $\tilde V(\cdot|\lambda)$ uniquely solves the relaxed Pareto problem;
* $\tilde U(\lambda)$ is continuous and strictly increasing;
* the relaxed cost-minimization solution for reservation utility $\bar U$ is found by choosing $\lambda$ so that $\tilde U(\lambda)=\bar U$, except on the low-reservation flat region where $\lambda=0$.

The proof of strict monotonicity is unchanged. If two different $\lambda$'s gave the same utility, convexity of the wage cost would contradict Pareto optimality. This does not depend on continuous output or unbounded utility.

---

## 6. The upper feasible reservation utility

The old proof uses $\tilde U(\lambda)\to\infty$. This is no longer true when utility is bounded. Define instead

$$
\bar U_R:=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\tilde U(\lambda)
\in\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}.
$$

Because $\tilde U$ is increasing, the limit exists.

If utility is unbounded, the current argument still implies $\bar U_R=\infty$. If utility is bounded, then

$$
\bar U_R<\bar u-c(a_0)<\infty.
$$

Interpretation: $\bar U_R$ is the maximum relaxed utility level attainable while satisfying LIC. It is approached as the marginal cost multiplier $\lambda$ becomes infinite.

We do not need an explicit formula for $\bar U_R$ in the main text. A short remark can say that, when utility is bounded, $\bar U_R$ is the value of a simple linear problem over utility contracts with range $[u(0),\bar u]$ and the LIC constraint. The extremal contracts use the full utility range and are step-like in the score.

---

## 7. Revised statement of Proposition 1

A clean generalized statement would be:

**Proposition 1, generalized.** Suppose the model satisfies the common-support dominated-density assumptions, the regularity assumptions needed for differentiation under the integral sign, and the implementability condition (Imp). Then there exist reservation utility levels

$$
\bar U_L<\bar U_R\le\infty
$$

such that:

1. For every $\bar U<\bar U_R$, the relaxed cost-minimization problem has an almost everywhere unique solution.
2. The solution is canonical: there exist multipliers $\lambda^*(\bar U)\ge0$ and $\mu^*(\bar U)>0$ such that

$$
v^*(y|\bar U)=V(y|\lambda^*(\bar U),\mu^*(\bar U))
\quad\nu\text{-a.e.}
$$

3. For $\bar U\le \bar U_L$, $\lambda^*(\bar U)=0$, and the relaxed optimal contract and optimal expected wage are constant in $\bar U$.
4. For $\bar U\in(\bar U_L,\bar U_R)$, $\lambda^*(\bar U)$ is strictly increasing.
5. As reservation utility approaches the upper feasible frontier,

$$
\lambda^*(\bar U)\to\infty
\quad\text{as}\quad
\bar U\uparrow\bar U_R.
$$

6. The optimal expected wage $\omega(\bar U)$ is strictly increasing and strictly convex on $(\bar U_L,\bar U_R)$.

This nests the current proposition because, under unbounded utility, $\bar U_R=\infty$.

---

## 8. Relationship to the main concavity theorem

This proposition should be stated more generally than the concavity theorem. Existence and characterization of the relaxed solution use only convexity, common support, the score, and local implementability. They do not use:

* ordered tail conditions;
* score normalization;
* $f_{aa}>0$ in a left tail;
* the high-reservation concavity mechanism.

The main theorem can then be phrased as:

* if $\bar u=\infty$, FOA is valid for all sufficiently high $\bar U$;
* if $\bar u<\infty$, FOA is valid for all $\bar U$ sufficiently close to $\bar U_R$ from below, under the stronger capacity/tail conditions from the concavity plan.

Thus Proposition 1 gives the canonical path and establishes that approaching the relevant high-reservation frontier is equivalent to

$$
\lambda^*(\bar U)\to\infty.
$$

The concavity theorem then studies the asymptotic behavior of these canonical contracts as $\lambda\to\infty$.

## 9. Discrete distributions and Poisson

The generalized Proposition 1 naturally covers Poisson output.

For Poisson with mean parameter depending on action, the output space is $\mathcal Y=\mathbb N$, and $\nu$ is counting measure. The density is the probability mass function

$$
f(y|a)=\Pr(Y=y|a).
$$

All integrals become sums:

$$
\int h(y)f(y|a)\,d\nu(y)
=
\sum_{y=0}^\infty h(y)f(y|a).
$$

The score is well-defined on the common support, and the canonical formula is unchanged:

$$
V(y|\lambda,\mu)=g(\lambda+\mu S(y|a_0)).
$$

Nothing in Proposition 1 requires the output support to be an interval. The only important modeling restriction is common support across actions.

For the concavity result, we can still include discrete examples by writing conditions in score-cutoff form, e.g.

$$
\{y:S(y|a_0)\le q\}
$$

instead of intervals such as $(-\infty,q]$. This avoids notation tied to continuous output and handles Poisson without much additional complexity.

## 10. Drafting choices for the paper

Recommended changes in the paper:

1. In the model section, define output on $\mathcal Y\subseteq\mathbb R$ with density $f(\cdot|a)$ relative to a common measure $\nu$.
2. Replace Lebesgue integrals by $d\nu(y)$ integrals in the model and Proposition 1 proof. In prose, say we continue to write integrals for both sums and ordinary integrals.
3. Change the utility assumption to allow $\bar u\le\infty$.
4. Add the local implementability condition (Imp), perhaps only before Proposition 1.
5. Revise Proposition 1 to use $\bar U_R$ instead of $\infty$.
6. Add a short remark explaining that when $\bar u=\infty$, $\bar U_R=\infty$ and the proposition reduces to the current statement.
7. Add a short remark explaining that when $\bar u<\infty$, $\bar U_R$ is the maximum feasible relaxed utility and $\lambda^*(\bar U)\to\infty$ as $\bar U\uparrow\bar U_R$.
8. Keep the concavity theorem conceptually separate: it adds the stronger score-tail/capacity assumptions needed to show global incentive compatibility near the upper frontier.

---

## 11. Clean limit display for final proof

Use this display in the final proof:

$$
\lim_{\mu\to\infty}g(\lambda+\mu S(y|a_0))
=
\begin{cases}
\bar u, & S(y|a_0)>0,\\
g(\lambda), & S(y|a_0)=0,\\
u(0), & S(y|a_0)<0.
\end{cases}
$$

Then, because $S=0$ states do not contribute to incentive,

$$
\lim_{\mu\to\infty}I(\lambda,\mu)
=
(\bar u-u(0))\int_{\{S>0\}}S f_0\,d\nu.
$$

This is the only new argument needed to replace the current unbounded-utility proof.
